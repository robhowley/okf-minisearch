# Crop Rotation

Crop rotation moves related crops between beds each year. It reduces recurring soil pests and diseases while balancing nutrient demand. Rotation works best when the plan tracks plant families, not just common crop names.

## Group Crops by Family

Keep tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, and potatoes together as nightshades; group cabbage, kale, and radishes as brassicas; and group beans and peas as legumes. Cucumbers, squash, and melons are cucurbits. Note perennial crops separately because they should not move each year. A family-labeled bed map is easier to follow.

## Use a Simple Sequence

Move heavy-feeding fruiting crops away from the same bed for at least two seasons when space allows. Follow them with legumes or a cover crop, then use the bed for leafy crops or roots. Rotation is not a cure for contaminated soil, and it cannot replace sanitation, resistant varieties, or good airflow, but it lowers repeated exposure.

## Coordinate the Garden

Record this year’s families at the shed and copy the map before winter cleanup. If plots are managed separately, coordinate adjacent beds so a disease is not simply moved a few feet away. When space is tight, rotate the most vulnerable families first and use containers or fresh soil for crops that cannot move. Review harvest and disease notes before choosing next year’s placements.
